About Ducks on Demand

Our (made-up) story

Ducks on Demand is a fictional store. In our imaginary story, it began as a shelf of toy ducks in a small shop by a pond. The shop, the owners and the story are all invented for this demo. No real business is described here, and nothing on this site is for sale.

A short history of the rubber duck

Rubber bath toys are older than most people think. Natural rubber was used for toys in the nineteenth century, and the process called vulcanization, patented by Charles Goodyear in 1844, made rubber far more durable. Later, in the 1940s, the sculptor Peter Ganine received a patent for a duck-shaped toy that floated. Today most rubber ducks are made from vinyl or other plastics rather than natural rubber, but the name has stayed.

The classic duck has a round body, a flat base that helps it float, and a bright yellow colour. It is a cheerful shape, and many makers have copied it in different sizes, colours and costumes.

Rubber duck debugging

Programmers know the rubber duck for another reason. In rubber duck debugging, a developer explains their code, line by line, to a toy duck. Saying the problem out loud often reveals the mistake. The idea appears in the book The Pragmatic Programmer, published in 1999, and many developers still keep a duck on the desk.

Giant ducks and duck races

Rubber ducks also appear in big public events. In 2007 the Dutch artist Florentijn Hofman made a giant floating duck sculpture, and versions of it have since travelled to many cities. Charity duck races, where thousands of numbered toy ducks are dropped into a river, are held in many places around the world.

Bath toys today

Modern bath toys must meet safety rules, and good ones are made so that they do not trap water or break into small parts. A duck is still a simple way to make bath time more fun for children, and a good reminder to take a short break.
